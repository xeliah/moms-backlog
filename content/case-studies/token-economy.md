## Problem

For about two months, our daughter (she was two and a half) held her poop for up to five days at a time. I know how that sentence reads. It started around a family trip to Menorca, where she came down with the flu and was just... a lot the whole time, crankier and more oppositional than usual. The withholding kicked off not long after we got home.

Here's the thing about holding it for five days: it's actually dangerous. It comes with its own special hell: chills, sweating, stomach pain that gets worse the longer it goes on. We were dealing with a kid in real physical distress, crying, not sleeping, day and night. Fun times.

## Why the standard approaches failed

We threw everything at it. Reassurance, poop-themed books and songs (yes, they exist, no they didn't help), talking about it at every possible moment of the day, even infant massage (there's a whole method for this called AIMI, we tried it). Her pediatrician put her on Macrogol to soften things up. None of it worked. She'd flat-out refuse to sit on the toilet, or sit for two seconds and pop back up going "there's nothing there."

The thing that actually moved the needle wasn't a bathroom trick at all. It was asking the right person. A pelvic-floor obstetrician I'd worked with during potty training pointed me toward a psychologist instead of more physical fixes. Our psychologist, who specializes in childhood behavior, is the one who suggested a token economy.

## Discovery

Before I built anything, I just watched. When was the resistance worse? Almost always at home, barely ever at daycare. Worse in the evenings, when she was tired. It wasn't one clean cause: there was real physical discomfort from the constipation and the withholding itself, plus a stretch of looser stools afterward that were still uncomfortable. There was also a very clear "I don't want to" in there. She said it herself, flat out: "I don't want to poop."

Our psychologist explained why rewards work here: when a kid is in a total behavioral standoff, you're not bribing them into good behavior, you're breaking the loop to get one small win. Once the habit sticks, the rewards fade out. They're a bridge, not a permanent fixture.

## Solution design

One clear trigger earns a token, the whole routine, start to finish: going, cleaning up, flushing, washing hands. Not sitting down. Not almost. The whole thing.

Three tokens = one reward. We mixed it up: homemade stickers, blowing bubbles together, an ice cream run. She picked the rewards herself, so it actually felt like hers, not just a prize I'd picked for her.

The board itself: a hand-drawn "potty jar" with three numbered slots and an arrow pointing up to a wrapped gift box. Fill the jar, unlock the box. A second sheet had poop-shaped tokens cut to fit the slots exactly, and a third had a handful of reward icons she picked from before we even started, so the goal was visible and locked in from day one.

## The product

It's laminated cardstock, hung in her bedroom, on purpose, so the reward moment is separate from the toilet itself. I hold onto the tokens. Once she's finished the whole routine, I hand her one, we walk to her room together, and she sticks it on the board herself. Everything's cut with a Silhouette Cameo and laminated so it can survive daily toddler handling.

*A cleaned-up, print-ready version of this board, with written instructions, is in progress as a downloadable toolkit.*

## Results

The day we introduced the board, she'd already been on Macrogol for a few days and had two spontaneous wins that same day: one at home, one at her grandparents'. Her body might've already been loosening up on its own, credit where it's due. But the moment we explained the system to her, she ran to the bathroom on her own, went, and earned her first token. That part was immediate.

Since then, she's gone almost every day, sometimes more than once. I want to be honest about what this actually is: one week of consistent results, with a confound (the Macrogol, plus those two wins before the system even started) that makes it hard to credit the token economy alone for the breakthrough. What it clearly did do is kill the resistance: she wants to go now, and goes to get her token. No relapses yet, but a week is a week, not a verdict.

## Retro

If I did this over, I wouldn't spend two months on reassurance and songs and massage first. I'd go straight to a psychologist who specializes in kid behavior. That was the actual lever. Everything before it was well-meant but not it.

The system isn't static. I'm already loosening it. She's going almost daily now, so I bumped the threshold from 3 tokens to 5, and the plan for the next month or two is to keep stretching the ratio, watch for any backslide, and eventually retire the board entirely once the habit holds on its own without it.

What I'm still watching: whether this holds once the novelty wears off, and whether the Macrogol timing means I need more runway before I call this a real win instead of a lucky week.
