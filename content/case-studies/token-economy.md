## Problem

For about two months, our daughter — then two and a half — held her stool for as long as five days at a time. It started around a family holiday in Menorca, where she came down with the flu; she was unusually irritable and oppositional throughout the trip, and the withholding began not long after.

The physical toll built with each day she held it: chills, sweating, stomach pain, worsening the longer it went on. That created a second problem on top of the first — the crying fits, irritability, and broken sleep that came with the physical discomfort meant we weren't just managing a bathroom issue, we were managing a child in real distress, day and night.

## Why the standard approaches failed

We tried the obvious things first: reassurance, poop-themed songs and books, talking about it at different points in the day, even infant massage techniques (AIMI method). Her pediatrician prescribed Macrogol to soften her stool. None of it moved the needle — she'd refuse to sit on the toilet at all, or sit for a second and immediately stand back up, insisting "there's nothing there."

The turning point wasn't a bathroom fix — it was asking the right specialist. A pelvic-floor obstetrician I'd worked with on early potty training pointed me toward psychological support instead of more physical techniques. Our psychologist, who specializes in childhood behavior, is the one who suggested a token economy.

## Discovery

Before starting anything, I paid attention to when the resistance was worse. It was almost always at home, rarely at daycare — and worse in the evening, when she was tired. It looked like a mix of causes, not one clean explanation: real physical discomfort from constipation and prolonged withholding, later followed by a few days of looser stools that were still uncomfortable and painful. But there was also a clear voluntary component — she'd say outright, "I don't want to poop."

Our psychologist explained the logic behind the reward-based approach for cases like this: when a child is in a total behavioral block, rewards aren't there to bribe compliance — they're there to break the loop and get a first success, however small. Once the habit is established, the rewards taper off; they're a bridge, not a permanent fixture.

## Solution design

We set one clear trigger for a token: full evacuation, plus the entire routine around it — going, cleaning up, flushing, washing up. Not sitting down, not attempting — completing the whole sequence.

Three tokens earned one reward. Rewards were a mix of tangible and experiential: homemade stickers, blowing bubbles together, an ice cream trip — chosen with her, not just for her, so the payoff felt personal rather than generic.

The board itself was designed to make progress visible at a glance: a drawn "potty jar" with three numbered, dashed slots, and an arrow pointing up to a wrapped gift box — fill the three slots, unlock the reward. A separate sheet held identical cut-out poop tokens sized to match the slots exactly, and another sheet held a small set of reward icons to cut out and choose from before starting, so the goal was fixed and visible from day one.

## The product

The board is laminated cardstock, hung in her bedroom rather than the bathroom — the reward moment is deliberately separate from the toilet itself. I hold the tokens; once she's finished the full routine, I hand her one, and we walk to her room together where she sticks it on the board herself. Tokens and reward icons are cut with a Silhouette Cameo and laminated the same way, so the whole system holds up to daily handling.

*A cleaned-up, print-ready version of this board — including written instructions — is in progress as a downloadable toolkit.*

## Results

The day we introduced the token board, she'd already been on Macrogol for several days and had two spontaneous evacuations that same day — one at home in the morning, one at her grandparents' in the afternoon. So her body may have already been loosening up on its own. What happened next, though, was immediate: the moment we explained the system, she ran to the bathroom on her own, went, and earned her first token.

Since then, she's gone almost every day, sometimes more than once. It's worth being precise about what this is: one week of consistent results, with a confound (Macrogol, plus two evacuations before the system even started) that makes it hard to credit the token economy alone for the initial breakthrough. What it does show clearly is that the system removed the resistance — she wants to go now, and goes to get her token. No relapses yet, but a week isn't long enough to call this settled.

## Retro

If I were starting over, I wouldn't spend two months on reassurance, books, and massage first — I'd bring in a psychologist specializing in childhood behavior right away. That's the step that actually worked; everything before it was well-intentioned but not the right lever.

The system itself isn't static — I'm already diluting it. With her going almost daily, I've moved the threshold from 3 tokens to 5 per reward, and the plan over the next 1-2 months is to keep loosening the ratio and monitoring for any regression, with the goal of retiring the board entirely once the habit holds without it.

What this doesn't solve, and what I'm still watching: whether the improvement holds once the novelty wears off, and whether the confound from the Macrogol timing means the "win" needs a longer runway before I'd call it validated.
